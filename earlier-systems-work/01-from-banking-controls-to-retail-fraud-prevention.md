# Earlier Systems Work: From Banking Controls to Retail Fraud Prevention

## Why this is here

Most of this portfolio documents work done with modern AI systems.

This example predates generative AI and matters for a different reason: it shows the same underlying problem-solving pattern without an AI in the loop.

At Harry Ritchie's Jewelers, a fraud incident occurred in my store and put one of my strongest salespeople at risk of being held accountable for a failure the company had never actually trained employees to prevent.

When I raised the issue with the CEO, my argument was straightforward:

> You cannot fairly hold an employee accountable for a control the company never taught them to perform.

The company did not have formal training for credible ID verification, physical inspection of identification documents, counterfeit-currency checks, or practical card-fraud prevention.

I had previously worked in banking, where fraud controls, identity verification, transaction risk, and compliance were ordinary parts of the operating environment.

So I adapted that knowledge to a very different context.

## The transfer

The problem was not simply "teach jewelry employees bank procedures."

A luxury retail store has different transactions, tools, customer expectations, risks, and failure modes.

The useful work was deciding which concepts transferred and turning them into something employees could actually use at the counter.

I built a practical training program covering:

- physical ID inspection and photo comparison;
- use of reference materials for state ID security features;
- blacklight and magnification checks;
- counterfeit-currency warning signs and security features;
- card-present transaction rules and failure handling;
- behavioral red flags during suspicious transactions;
- when to stop, decline, or escalate a transaction;
- documentation of verification steps in credit applications.

The surviving training deck is titled **Fraud Prevention 101: Quick ID, Currency Checking and Card Fraud Tutorial**.

## From observation to control

The sequence was:

1. A real fraud event exposed a failure.
2. The initial response focused on employee accountability.
3. I challenged the assumption behind that response: the required skill had never been trained.
4. I recognized that knowledge from a previous industry could address the gap.
5. I translated banking controls into a retail-specific procedure.
6. I turned that procedure into teachable coursework rather than leaving it as informal advice.
7. I raised the control gap and proposed response to executive leadership.
8. The company initially purchased blacklight inspection equipment for stores, and I trained my own staff on the procedure.

The company did not consistently maintain the training program or keep the supporting ID-reference materials current over the long term. That part was outside my control.

I include that limitation because the useful claim is not that I personally transformed company-wide fraud operations forever. The useful claim is that I identified a systemic gap, made the accountability problem legible, transferred relevant knowledge across domains, and built an operational response that people could use.

## What this demonstrates

- Cross-disciplinary problem solving
- Systems thinking before generative AI
- Translating specialist knowledge for frontline users
- Risk and control design
- Procedural fairness and accountability
- Executive communication
- Training and operational implementation
- Distinguishing the problem I could solve from the organizational follow-through I did not control

## Why it connects to my current work

The surface area is completely different from AI product work, but the reasoning pattern is familiar:

**notice a failure -> question the assumed explanation -> identify a missing system or control -> borrow useful concepts from another domain -> adapt them to the actual environment -> make the rule explicit -> turn it into something people can use.**

That pattern did not begin when I started using GPT, Claude, or Gemini.

The AI systems made it possible for me to apply the same kind of thinking to much more technical product problems. They did not invent the underlying habit.
